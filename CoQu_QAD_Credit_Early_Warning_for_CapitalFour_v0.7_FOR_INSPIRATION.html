How the model compresses 150 fundamentals

Each issuer quarter starts as up to 150 reported fundamentals. The pipeline turns them into one risk score in the steps below.

Step 1 · fundamentals

Step 3 · independent directions
58–96

  1. Size-free ratios and trends. Each reported item is divided by total assets or turned into a year-on-year change, so the model reacts to the financial shape of an issuer, not to its size.
  2. Measured against healthy history. Every indicator is standardised on how it is distributed among healthy issuers in the training period, so a value reads as “how unusual is this for a healthy company”.
  3. Collapsed onto independent directions. Many indicators move together. A principal-component step rewrites them as a smaller set of independent directions, each a fixed mix of indicators.
  4. Ordering rules keep faint warning directions. A standard compression keeps only the directions with the most variation. Warning signals often sit in faint, low-variance directions, so the pipeline uses ordering rules that can keep those directions too.
  5. Quantum kernel: similarity to healthy quarters. Each quarter’s kept directions are encoded into a quantum state. The kernel measures how similar that state is to healthy reference quarters; low similarity means the quarter looks unusual.
  6. Autoencoder: compress and reconstruct. A small network learns to compress healthy profiles and rebuild them. A quarter it rebuilds poorly does not look like a healthy issuer.
  7. One risk score. The two signals, together with a third check of how unusual each indicator is on its own, are calibrated and combined into a single risk score for every issuer quarter.
  8. An alarm line with a fixed false-alarm budget. The alarm line is set so that at most 5% or 10% of healthy quarters in the tuning cohort trip it. It is then applied unchanged to issuers the model has never seen.

Why the ordering matters. The chart shows how much variation each independent direction carries (bars) and the running total (line). Of the 8 directions that best separate pre-event quarters from healthy ones (marked), 4 lie beyond the first 58 directions that a standard compression to 80% of the variation would keep. This is why the pipeline also orders directions by warning signal, not only by size.

Reading and limitations

The test cohort holds 33 distressed issuers with at least one quarter in the warning window, against 147 healthy issuers. With so few events the 95% intervals are wide: for the share of distressed issuers caught at the 5% line the interval runs 24–61%. 26 of the 67 events used (12 among the test issuers) have a partly confirmed date: the event is documented, but its exact day rests on fewer agencies or on secondary reports. The model is still only partly tuned; further tuning rounds may still improve it. The tuning started from settings carried over from the first version, in which some of today’s test issuers belonged to the tuning cohort; their influence on this result is indirect, but not zero. The first stage has been tuned for one of three quantum-kernel types so far. Every figure should be read as direction, not validated performance; a pilot on a larger issuer universe with agency rating histories would turn these indications into measured performance.

How the model can still be improved

This is a pre-release model and it is still only partly tuned. There are 14 ways it can still be tuned or improved, in four groups. Any change is tested on held-out issuers before a result is replaced.

Make the flags more reliable (fewer false alarms)

  1. A level that separates good flags from bad ones. A level that actually separates good flags from bad ones would have to be built and tested in the next run, for example from how long a flag persists or how many indicator families move together, not from score height alone. On this sample the 1–5 levels based on score height do not yet do this: flags at levels 1, 2 and 3 were right in 25%, 26%, 24% of held-out cases.
  2. Tune directly for flags that are right. Today 25% of flagged issuers were right (14 correct warnings, 43 false alarms). The search rewards how many distressed issuers are caught and how early, with a smaller penalty for healthy issuers flagged; weighting false alarms more heavily trades some detection for cleaner flags.
  3. Confirmation rules. Raise a flag only when it holds on consecutive filings, or when indicators from several families move together, and test on held-out issuers which rule removes the most false alarms for the fewest missed events.
  4. An alarm line set to your trade-off. The line now lets at most 5% of healthy quarters through (10% is shown as well). Where to put it depends on the cost of a missed event versus the cost of a false alarm, which can be set with you.

Finish tuning the current model

  1. Run the search until it settles. In the last tuning round the best settings were still improving, so the model is still only partly tuned; further rounds continue until the improvement stops.
  2. The remaining quantum-kernel types. The first stage has been tuned for one of its three quantum-kernel types; the other two are still to be tuned.
  3. More re-checks of the best settings. Re-check the leading settings over more independent runs and more resampled issuer groups, so the choice rests on many cases rather than a few.
  4. Issuer- and time-robust validation. Validate across resampled issuer groups and separate time periods, so the chosen settings hold beyond one split of issuers and one stretch of the credit cycle.

Better inputs

  1. More credit events and a wider issuer universe. This version uses 67 events. More events — including agency rating histories with outlooks and watch placements, and your own lists — give the tuning more to learn from and narrow the ranges around every figure.
  2. Richer indicators. Add multi-quarter trends and changes, comparisons with sector peers, and market information such as equity prices and bond spreads alongside the filed fundamentals.
  3. Stronger point-in-time data. Confirm the 26 event dates that are only partly confirmed, and reduce filled-in values, so every signal reflects exactly what was known at the time.
  4. The length of the warning window. The window is 24 months. A shorter window (12 or 18 months) asks for later but sharper warnings; the right length depends on how you act on a flag.

Model structure

  1. Separate views for defaults and for downgrades, and by sector. Defaults and downgrades to high yield, and different sectors, show distress in different fundamentals; separate or sector-aware models can sharpen each.
  2. Combination with your own screens. Flags raised both by the quantum early warning and by your own screens can be tested as a combined, higher-confidence signal.
